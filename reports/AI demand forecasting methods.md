# Simulate AI's money flows, not its trend lines

No single forecasting method can model AI infrastructure demand from 2026 to 2030. The best engine for a browser tool built only on public data is a layered hybrid. At its core is a **double-entry ledger of about 40 named firms**: AI labs, hyperscalers, neoclouds, financing vehicles, Nvidia and its suppliers, and lenders. Each firm follows **simple behavioural rules taken from game and decision theory**, and supply moves through **capacity pipelines with realistic lead times**. Every quarter a **credit-and-contagion step** settles who can pay whom, and a **Monte Carlo loop** runs thousands of possible futures. Time-series and technology-curve models should feed that engine and keep it honest. Their jobs are to estimate the current quarter, measure the odds that a downturn has started, and set ranges on how fast token prices fall. They should not be trusted to forecast turning points in 2027–2030: private-sector consensus forecasts caught only **5 of 153 recessions** a year ahead. History explains why the engine has to be built around financing. In railways, telecom, shale and shipping, **the bust began when financing that had to be renewed met capacity commitments made on extrapolated demand**, and the suppliers that had financed their own customers lost the most. Lucent's revenue fell about 60%, against about 15% for Cisco. Only agent, game and network models can represent those mechanics: who owes whom, who cuts spending first, and which lender refuses to roll over a loan. The engine is cheap to run. In a team benchmark, **2,000 futures × 20 quarters × 40 firms took 0.24–1.5 seconds** in Chrome's JavaScript engine, so users can move sliders and see results live. The tool's front page should lead with financing indicators, and several were flashing by late 2026. The main weakness is data. Many 2026 inputs, including Nvidia's latest quarter, Anthropic's run-rate, Nvidia's roughly $118B of guarantees and TSMC's packaging capacity, came only from secondary sources and must be checked against filings before launch.

## History says busts start in the financing, not the demand curve

The first thing to settle is what the model must be able to reproduce, and the historical record is unusually consistent. Odlyzko calls Britain's 1840s Railway Mania "by many measures the greatest technology mania in history." Investors' implied expectations for 1850 railway revenue were **£30–50M, while actual industry revenue was £4.5–6.2M**, a gap of 5–8x. That is the Victorian version of today's "$600B question." The mania went ahead even though it had "many powerful, vocal, and insightful critics" ([Odlyzko](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/hallucinations.pdf); [Odlyzko GMU talk](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/talks/gmu2008.pdf)). In American railroads, the marginal financier left before the defaults: European capital pulled back after the 1890 Baring crisis, and the receivership wave began in 1893 ([Panic of 1893](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1893); [MarketHistories](https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-panic-of-1893-how-silver-and-railroad-debt-sparked-americas-worst-depression-yet)). In the 1920s, Insull's utility empire stacked holding companies up to "ten times removed from the operating company." The power plants kept running, but the financial superstructure collapsed in 1932 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/insull-utilities-trusts-collapse)).

The telecom and fibre boom of 1996–2002 is the closest modern analog. It was fed by the claim that internet traffic "doubles every 100 days," which traces to WorldCom/UUNET. Odlyzko and Coffman measured actual growth of **about 100% a year**, a doubling roughly once a year rather than roughly twelvefold ([Odlyzko](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/itcom.internet.growth.pdf)). US telecom capex peaked at about $120B in 2000 ([Quartz](https://qz.com/ai-debt-dot-com-telecom-borrowing-comparison-050526)). The capex-to-revenue ratio reached about **4x, against roughly 6–7x for AI data-centre spending versus AI revenue in 2025** ([Derek Thompson](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/why-ai-is-not-a-bubble), secondary). Global Crossing and WorldCom went bankrupt, and almost every major telecom restated its 1999–2000 accounts after WorldCom capitalised operating costs ([Fabricated Knowledge](https://www.fabricatedknowledge.com/p/lessons-from-history-the-rise-and)). Equipment suppliers show the clearest result. Lucent ran a vendor-financing book of about $8.4B and pulled orders forward with discounts, and its revenue fell from about **$30B to about $12B in two years** ([Lazonick & March](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/22012/1/lazonick-march_lucent_final_20100410.pdf); [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1366438/lucent-misses-q2-mark-at-t-sees-higher-earnings.html)). Cisco had little vendor financing and a large enterprise business, and its revenue fell only **about 15%** ([Carpenter & Lazonick, INET](https://www.ineteconomics.org/uploads/papers/WP_202-Carpenter-and-Lazonick-Cisco-final.pdf)). For an Nvidia model, the deciding variable is therefore how much of its demand Nvidia is financing or guaranteeing itself.

Shale and shipping show the same pattern from other angles. Surviving shale producers spent about twice their cash flow in 2015–16, and more than 100 bankruptcies followed the oil-price collapse ([Oilprice.com](https://oilprice.com/Energy/Energy-General/Why-Cheap-Shale-Gas-Will-End-Soon.html); [Kirk Coburn](https://kirkcoburn.com/2020/08/27/the-us-shale-industry-from-boom-to-bust/)). In dry-bulk shipping, the orderbook (ships on order as a share of ships afloat) climbed from 58% in March 2008 to **69.8% in September 2008**, just before freight rates collapsed ([Star Bulk F-3/A, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001386716/000091957408005968/d904060a_f-3.htm)). Greenwood and Hanson's study of shipping from 1976 to 2011 gives the behavioural mechanism. High earnings coincided with heavy investment but predicted *low* future returns, because owners extrapolated demand and underweighted their rivals' supply response, which the authors call "competition neglect" ([NBER w19246](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19246)).

Two differences from these analogs matter for model design. First, **GPUs lose most of their economic value in 3–6 years**, while fibre and rail last decades. "Build now, demand arrives later" therefore produces write-offs rather than dormant assets that can be switched on cheaply later. Shells, grid connections and power contracts behave more like fibre conduit, so the model should carry the two asset types separately ([CNBC on GPU depreciation](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/14/ai-gpu-depreciation-coreweave-nvidia-michael-burry.html)). Second, AI capacity still looked tight in 2026, not glutted: one-year H100 rental prices *rose* about 40% between October 2025 and March 2026 ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/the-great-gpu-shortage-rental-capacity?open=false)). The bull case that hyperscalers fund capex from cash flow ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-research/ai-in-a-bubble/report.pdf)) is eroding. In Q2 2026, capex exceeded operating cash flow at Alphabet (about 115%) and Amazon (about 119%) ([FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/hyperscalers-tap-external-financing-as-ai-capex-outruns-cash-flow)). The Bank of England's FPC cites roughly **$450B of AI-related debt issuance by early September 2026** and JPMorgan's estimate of **$4.1T of debt-financed capex for 2026–30** ([BoE FPC Record](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/financial-policy-committee-record/2026/september-2026)).

The modelling lesson is that "bubble or not" is the wrong question. In every analog the physical capacity was eventually used and the financiers were wiped out. The tool has to answer a narrower question: **when does financing turn, and how large is the capacity overshoot at that moment?** Any method that cannot represent contracts, debt maturities, lead times and supplier financing cannot answer it. That is the test applied to each method family below. The research found few dot-com-specific (as opposed to telecom) or shale game-theory sources, so those two analogs are thinner than the others.

## Trend-fitting and judgment detect turns late but set honest ranges

### Econometric and time-series models track trends and miss turns

**What they are.** A *VAR* (vector autoregression) predicts several series together, such as hyperscaler capex, cloud revenue and Nvidia data-centre revenue, from their own and each other's recent past. A *Bayesian VAR* adds prior beliefs so that a short history does not produce wild estimates. *BSTS* (Bayesian structural time series, from Google's Scott and Varian) splits a series into trend, seasonality and the effect of many candidate leading indicators, and automatically keeps only the indicators that help ([Scott & Varian](https://people.ischool.berkeley.edu/%7Ehal/Papers/2013/pred-present-with-bsts.pdf)). A *regime-switching* model assumes the world flips between hidden states, such as "boom" and "contraction," and reports the probability that it is in each state now.

**Track record.** Standard forecasts follow trends well and almost never call turns. An IMF study of 63 countries found GDP forecasts are revised too slowly even in recession years ([IMF WP 18/39](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2018/03/05/how-well-do-economists-forecast-recessions-45672)), and only **5 of 153 recessions were predicted by the consensus a year ahead** ([BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/why-are-economists-so-bad-at-forecasting-recessions-1.1250945)). Semiconductor-industry forecasts behave the same way, with revisions that move toward the outcome but arrive late. WSTS cut its 2005 forecast from 8.5% to 1.2% and deepened 2019 from −12.1% to −13.3% ([WSTS 2004](https://www.wsts.org/76/103/WSTS-Semiconductor-Market-Forecast-Autumn-2004); [Electronic Specifier](https://www.electronicspecifier.com/news/analysis/the-semiconductor-market-decline-in-2019/)). In 2018, most forecasters called 6–8% growth against an actual 13.7% ([Semiconductor Intelligence](https://us.design-reuse.com/industryexpertblogs/31489/semiconductor-forecast.html)). The current upcycle shows the same under-calling of a boom. Consensus for Nvidia's FY2027 data-centre revenue was $306.5B in January 2026, and a May note put the FY2027 total at $343.4B. The two figures may not measure the same thing ([S&P Global Jan](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2026/01/nvidia-at-ces-2026); [S&P Global May](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2026/05/nvidia-earnings-preview-q1-2027)). The practical lesson is that the *direction and momentum of consensus revisions* carry more information than the consensus level.

Regime-switching models are the best-documented tool for spotting a turn quickly *once it has started*. In real-time tests they dated US business-cycle peaks roughly 4–5 months before the NBER's official announcement, with few false alarms ([MPRA 60250](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/60250/8/MPRA_paper_60250.pdf); [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/works/2884539)). That lead is over the *announcement*, not over the turn itself. **The weakness for AI is sample size.** The AI era offers only about 12–14 quarterly observations, so any VAR leans heavily on its priors and on pre-AI data with different dynamics. No public study measures VAR or BSTS accuracy on hyperscaler capex or Nvidia revenue. All three are easy to run in a browser, since the data are hundreds of points, not millions. **Their role** is to estimate the current quarter (BSTS, using TSMC monthly revenue, Taiwan export orders and SEMI equipment billings), to supply a live "probability we are in a downturn regime" signal (regime switching on capex growth or chip billings), and to act as benchmarks. They should not be the source of 2028–2030 paths.

### Diffusion curves are unreliable before the midpoint; experience curves have known error bands

**What they are.** The *Bass model* and logistic or Gompertz *S-curves* describe adoption that starts slowly, accelerates and then saturates. *Experience curves* (Wright's law: cost falls a fixed percentage with each doubling of cumulative output) and *Moore-type trends* (cost falls a fixed percentage per year) describe price deflation.

**Track record.** S-curves are fragile before the inflection point. The data needed to pin down the Bass parameters typically arrive only **about two-thirds of the way to the adoption peak**, and earlier forecasts "can be wildly inaccurate" ([arXiv 2006.06373](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.06373)). The estimated market size tends to sit close to whatever cumulative adoption has been reached so far ([Van den Bulte & Lilien](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.16.4.338)). AI adoption in 2026 is plausibly still before that point: 56.8% of firms on the Ramp AI Index make a paid AI purchase, while the Census BTOS survey shows about 20% using AI ([Ramp](https://ramp.com/data/ai-index); [Census](https://census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)). A Bass fit today would mostly echo the latest data point. Experience curves are much better grounded. Farmer and Lafond fitted 53 technologies and showed that forecast errors collapse onto one **universal error distribution that widens predictably with horizon**, which gives calibrated ranges rather than point guesses ([Farmer & Lafond](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05274)). Wright's law and time-based Moore's law perform about equally ([Lafond et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.05979)). That matters because cumulative tokens served, the input Wright's law needs, is not public.

**Applying this to token prices takes care.** Epoch finds the price for a *fixed* capability level falls **9× to 900× a year**, depending on the benchmark ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/blog/llm-inference-price-trends)). The *effective* price users actually pay fell only about 6% year-to-date in 2026, because usage keeps shifting to frontier and reasoning models ([YipitData](https://www.yipitdata.com/resources/blog/cloud-llm-pricing-trends)). Running frontier models may even be getting dearer ([arXiv 2511.23455](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.23455), attribution unverified). Telecom gives the warning: traffic grew while bandwidth prices fell faster, so revenue lagged. **The engine should model revenue as volume × effective price**, with a Farmer–Lafond-style random walk for capability-adjusted price and a separate mix-shift parameter. S-curves belong only in scenarios, with a ceiling imposed from outside (for example a share of IT spend or labour cost) rather than estimated. All of these methods are trivial to run in a browser.

### Judgment, reference classes and markets supply priors and guardrails

**What they are.** *Superforecasting* is structured, aggregated human judgment, scored for accuracy. *Reference-class forecasting* (Kahneman, Flyvbjerg) starts from the outcomes of a set of similar past cases, the "outside view," before adjusting for specifics. *Prediction markets* (Kalshi, Polymarket, Metaculus) turn traders' bets into probabilities.

**Evidence.** The Good Judgment Project won IARPA's 2011–15 tournament and reportedly beat an intelligence-community prediction market by about 25–30%. Secondary accounts of the margin are inconsistent, but roughly 70% of superforecasters stayed in the top tier the following year ([AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/)). Flyvbjerg's "iron law" holds that 9 of 10 transport megaprojects overrun, with rail averaging 44.7%, and the pattern has not improved in 70 years ([Flyvbjerg et al.](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/4419156/mod_resource/content/1/volume%202.pdf)). In the M4 forecasting competition, **5 of the top 6 entries were combinations of methods** ([arXiv 2012.03854](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.03854)). M5's machine-learning wins came from tens of thousands of similar sales series ([Makridakis et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v38y2022i4p1346-1364.html)). AI demand offers a handful of short, unstable series, so the right lesson is to combine simple, diverse models rather than fit heavy machine learning. Prediction markets are of little use here. The only liquid contracts are near-certain short-horizon Nvidia events, such as a ~90–97% probability of beating earnings ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/05/14/will-nvidia-beat-q1-earnings-on-may-20-heres-what/); [Webull/Benzinga](https://www.webull.com/news/15469458290525184)). These encode Nvidia's habit of guiding low, not the level of demand. No contract on capex or AI revenue was found.

**Their role** is to set guardrails. Reference-class ranges from past overbuilds (railways, telecom, shipping, chip cycles) and megaproject overrun distributions are used to check whether the engine's 2030 ranges are implausibly narrow. Prediction-market and consensus probabilities serve as benchmarks that the tool's own probabilities can be scored against over time. A full base-rate dataset of tech-capex peak-to-trough drawdowns does not yet exist and will need a dedicated data pass.

## An accounting-disciplined agent model fits the problem

**What the four frameworks are.** An *agent-based model (ABM)* simulates individual named actors, such as OpenAI, Oracle, CoreWeave or a Blue Owl fund, each with its own balance sheet and decision rules, and lets the aggregate outcome emerge from their interactions. *System dynamics (SD)* treats an industry as tanks and pipes, with stocks such as installed GPUs and flows such as orders and deliveries, connected by feedback loops and delays. It is the classic tool for boom-bust capacity cycles. *Stock-flow consistent (SFC)* accounting is less a model than a discipline: every payment has a payer and a payee, and every asset is someone else's liability, so a loss cannot vanish. *Monte Carlo* means running a model thousands of times with randomly drawn inputs and reporting the spread of outcomes. It is a wrapper around a model, not a model of the economy.

**What each is good at, and where it fails.** SD captures the mechanism at the heart of the AI risk. Sterman's "Getting Big Too Fast" shows that **when demand moves fast relative to capacity-adjustment delays, forecasting errors produce excess capacity** that overwhelms the benefits of scale ([MIT Sloan](https://mitmgmtfaculty.mit.edu/jsterman/gettingbigtoofast/)). Fabs, HBM lines and power take 2–4 years to build, so this is the textbook setting. SD has been used on semiconductor cycles for decades. One example models the 2000–02 swing in which chip sales rose more than 35% and then fell almost as much ([Bezemer & Akkermans, ISDC 2003](https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2003/proceed/PAPERS/414.pdf)). Another found that planning capacity purely from demand trends produces "a significant amount of capacity overage" ([NTU](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/ntu-10356%2F158531)). SD's failure mode is that each stock is a homogeneous average. It cannot represent one neocloud's take-or-pay contract with one lab, or one GPU-backed loan. The ABM's failure modes are the reverse: too many free parameters and hard calibration ([Windrum, Fagiolo & Moneta](https://www.lem.sssup.it/WPLem/files/2006-14.pdf)). Rahmandad and Sterman's head-to-head comparison found that agent and aggregate models often give similar base cases, but **respond differently to policy and stress** ([MIT](https://www.mit.edu/~jsterman/Heterogeneity.html)). Stress counterfactuals, such as "what if lenders tighten covenants on CoreWeave-type borrowers," are exactly what fund managers will use this tool for. Monte Carlo is the most complete way to show uncertainty, and Damodaran argues best-case/worst-case analysis is "not very informative" ([Damodaran](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/DSV2/Ch3.pdf)). But Monte Carlo output is only as good as the input distributions ([Decimal Point Analytics](https://decimalpointanalytics.com/insights/articles/monte-carlo-machine-learning-equity-research)).

**Evidence of use.** ABMs are now standard complementary tools at central banks. A 2025 Bank of England-led survey ([BoE WP 1,122](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2025/agent-based-modeling-at-central-banks-recent-developments-and-new-challenges)) documents this, and its expanded 2026 version reportedly covers 24 central banks. The BoE's UK housing ABM is a close structural analog: a few agent types with heterogeneous balance sheets, lender limits similar to loan covenants, and spillovers between segments ([BoE SWP 619](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2016/macroprudential-policy-in-an-agent-based-model-of-the-uk-housing-market.pdf)). An Austrian ABM "can compete with" standard VAR and DSGE models in out-of-sample forecasting ([Poledna et al., IIASA](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/18339/1/1-s2.0-S0014292122001891-main%20%281%29.pdf)). The US Treasury's Office of Financial Research built a fire-sale ABM and found that **"the reaction to initial losses rather than the losses themselves" determines how big a crisis gets** ([OFR WP 14-05](https://www.financialresearch.gov/working-papers/2014/07/29/an-agent-based-model-for-financial-vulnerability/)). That argues for modelling lender behaviour inside the engine rather than treating it as an input. The hybrid is also established: Caiani, Stiglitz and co-authors built an *agent-based stock-flow consistent* benchmark that puts agent rules on top of an SFC ledger ([Caiani et al.](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/Joseph_Stiglitz/Agent%20based-stock%20flow.pdf)). No published AB-SFC model of a single industry value chain or of AI financing was found, so this design is an adaptation, not a replication.

**Scale makes the agent approach practical.** With about 40 named entities, this is a "named-entity" model, much closer to a disaggregated SD model than to a macro ABM with millions of agents. That sharply reduces the over-parameterisation problem, provided firms of the same type share rule templates and their differences come from real balance sheets rather than free behavioural knobs.

**Browser feasibility is not a constraint.** In a toy AB-SFC model (40 agents, a contract matrix, double-entry cash transfers, borrowing, defaults with 60% loss and random renegotiation), the research team ran **2,000 futures × 20 quarters in 237 milliseconds** with one pass over all agent pairs per quarter, 1.5 seconds with ten passes and 12.4 seconds with a hundred. This was an unpublished benchmark on Node 22, which uses the same engine as Chrome. It was not tested on Safari or Firefox. Monte Carlo runs are independent, so they can be split across the browser's background threads (Web Workers), which should bring a realistic model to well under a few seconds. Compiling to WebAssembly would buy only about 1.3× on average ([Jangda et al., USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/jangda)) and is not worth the complexity. Browser SD engines exist, such as SDEverywhere, which turns Vensim models into JavaScript ([GitHub](https://github-redirect.dependabot.com/climateinteractive/SDEverywhere)), and sd.js ([npm](https://npmjs.com/package/sd.js)). The JavaScript ABM library AgentScript is built for spatial, NetLogo-style models ([AgentScript](https://github.com/backspaces/agentscript)). Neither handles per-firm contracts and defaults, so a bespoke engine of about 1–2k lines of TypeScript is the pragmatic choice.

## Game and decision theory supply the behavioural rules at almost no cost

The agent layer needs rules for how each firm decides. Game theory and decision theory supply rules with real empirical grounding, and almost all of them reduce to a formula or a quick search for a threshold.

### Each type of player needs a different decision theory

**What they are.** *Expected utility* means choosing the option with the best probability-weighted payoff, the textbook rational investor. *Prospect theory* (Kahneman and Tversky) says people judge outcomes as gains or losses against a reference point, feel losses more than gains, and take *more* risk when below target. *Ambiguity aversion* applies when probabilities themselves are unknown: decision-makers then act as if the worst plausible case will occur.

**Evidence and fit.** Firms below their reference level take more risk than firms above it in COMPUSTAT data ([Kliger & Tsur](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/prospect-theory-and-risk-seeking-behavior-by-troubled-firms/)). The same holds across international listed firms ([RIBAF 2017](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/riibaf/v42y2017icp900-911.html)). The known weakness is that results depend on how the reference point is defined ([SciTePress](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2019/91993/91993.pdf)). Ambiguity lowers the price firms will pay for projects ([Izhakian](https://www.bwl.uni-mannheim.de/media/Lehrstuehle/bwl/Area_Finance/Finance_Area_Seminar/HWS_2019/Izhakian_Paper.pdf)), and German firms with higher beliefs about tail risk invest less, as if the worst case will happen ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/index%2Ephp/voxeu/columns/devil-tail-how-firms-beliefs-about-rare-macroeconomic-disasters-shape-investment)). Hyperscaler executives describe an asymmetric loss: Pichai reportedly said "the risk of underinvesting is dramatically greater than the risk of overinvesting" ([Invezz](https://invezz.com/news/2025/12/26/looking-ahead-to-2026-why-hyperscalers-cant-slow-spending-without-losing-the-ai-war/), second-hand; verify against the transcript). This suggests the following mapping. Model **hyperscalers** as expected-utility maximisers with a penalty for falling behind rivals. Model **cash-burning labs and levered neoclouds** as prospect-theory players whose reference point is their funding runway or the revenue path promised to investors. Below that point they "gamble for resurrection" by buying more compute. Model **lenders** as ambiguity-averse, with a worst-case haircut on GPU collateral. A sudden widening of spreads on news is the signature of ambiguity, not ordinary risk. No study tests the three theories head-to-head on corporate capex, so the choice for each player type is a judgment call.

### Capacity games explain why nobody cuts first

**What they are.** In *preemption games*, rivals rush to invest because waiting lets a competitor take the market. In a *war of attrition* (or "chicken game"), each firm keeps producing in a glut and hopes a rival cuts first.

**Evidence.** Grenadier shows that competition erodes the value of waiting, so firms invest **close to the zero-NPV threshold** ([Grenadier 2002](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v15y2002i3p691-721.html)). He also shows that building booms can rationally continue while demand falls ([Grenadier 1996](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v51y1996i5p1653-79.html)). Huisman and Kort extend this to choosing capacity size as well as timing ([RAND 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:randje:v:46:y:2015:i:2:p:376-408)). DRAM is the canonical chicken game: producers kept output high as prices fell, and Qimonda and Elpida exited ([CORE](https://core.ac.uk/works/128086389)). During the downturn Elpida raised capex 67% to catch the next upswing ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/769189/computers-elpida-sees-dram-prices-rising-next-year-lifts-spending.html)). A 2026 Korean report warns of a replay if AI demand slows while memory makers expand together ([Herald Corp](https://biz.heraldcorp.com/article/10892105)). **In the engine**, hyperscaler capex runs as a two-regime game. In boom quarters, the investment trigger falls as the number of serious rivals rises. In bust quarters, all firms delay cuts until a cash or credit constraint binds. Two cheap add-ons make overbuild emerge on its own. One is a Greenwood–Hanson parameter for how much of rivals' announced capacity each firm takes into account. The other is a term that makes each firm's capex respond to peers' capex surprises, following the Scharfstein–Stein herding logic ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/mit/sloanp/2213.html); only the bibliographic record was retrieved). The engine needs one more caution about market structure. Antitrust practice shows that the choice between Cournot (capacity-constrained), Bertrand (price war) and bargaining models changes results materially ([FTC WP 348](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/working_paper_348.pdf)). Electricity Cournot models also "vastly exceed observed prices" unless forward contracts are netted out ([Bushnell, Mansur & Saravia](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13507.pdf)). Long-term compute contracts are the AI equivalent of those forward contracts, so the engine should carry contracted volume as a state and could switch market regime with utilisation. The 85% utilisation threshold suggested in the notes is an unsourced proposal.

### Bargaining and strategic default predict renegotiation, not wholesale collapse

**What they are.** *Bargaining models* (Nash, Rubinstein) split a surplus according to each side's walk-away option and patience. *Strategic default* (Hart and Moore) says a borrower who can withdraw something essential can credibly owe only what the creditor would lose without it, so debt gets renegotiated down to the creditor's outside option ([Hart & Moore](https://hart.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/theory-debt-based-inalienability-human-capital)).

**Evidence.** The closest precedent for AI compute commitments is the 1980s US natural-gas take-or-pay crisis. Pipelines had signed long-term contracts at high prices in 1977–82. When prices fell, the liabilities were mostly resolved by negotiated buyouts. By 1986 about **$3.5B had been settled, about $6.5B of exposure remained, and about $1.4B was in litigation** ([OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/6840048)), while FERC Order 500 spread losses across producers and customers ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/26/1129/619030/)). AI shows the same early pattern. Microsoft reportedly voided about 200 MW of leases in February 2025, and about 2 GW of projects were abandoned by March ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-26/microsoft-abandons-more-data-center-projects-td-cowen-says)). Oracle and OpenAI reportedly dropped the Abilene expansion in March 2026, partly because OpenAI wanted newer Nvidia generations ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2026/03/07/openai-and-oracle-reportedly-abandon-tx-stargate-expansion/4159805)). In September 2026 Oracle reportedly invoked force majeure on a site funded by Blue Owl, although the lease is said to contain "hell-or-high-water" terms ([Pivot to AI](https://pivot-to-ai.com/2026/09/25/oracle-invokes-force-majeure-on-openai-data-centre-contract/), an opinionated secondary source; verify). **In the engine**, each contract carries its terms (take-or-pay share, termination fee, force-majeure flag), and each quarter a closed-form bargaining step decides whether the lab pays, delays or renegotiates. The result depends on the provider's ability to re-lease the capacity: strong for current-generation Nvidia GPUs, weak for bespoke or near-obsolete hardware. The gas-era base rate suggests the default assumption should be partial settlement, not full performance or total default, unless the counterparty is a single-asset vehicle.

### Global games turn lender runs into a computable threshold

**What they are.** In a *global game* (Morris and Shin), each lender sees a slightly noisy private signal of a borrower's health and rolls over the loan only if that signal is above a cutoff. The small amount of noise turns an unpredictable "everyone runs or nobody runs" situation into **one threshold** ([MIT 14.126 notes](https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/14.126-game-theory-spring-2024/mit14_126_s24_lecture_global-games.pdf)). Run probability then becomes a smooth function of fundamentals, which suits Monte Carlo well.

**Evidence.** The framework is standard for bank runs, currency attacks and rollover crises, and central banks use it ([Bank of Canada WP 2015-14](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/wp2015-14.pdf); [BIS WP 135](https://www.bis.org/publ/work135.pdf)). In dynamic versions, accumulated public news can produce **sudden runs after long calm periods** ([Angeletos, Hellwig & Pavan](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Angeletos%20Hellwig%20Pavan%20%28Ecma%202007%29.pdf)). That fits a refinancing wall in 2027–28. No paper applies global games to GPU-backed or equipment-lease credit specifically, so the noise level and cutoff have to be scenario settings. Nvidia's equity stakes and backstops can be modelled as signals that raise lenders' beliefs. A visible Nvidia withdrawal would then shift beliefs sharply.

### Approximate solution methods keep the games affordable

Solving a full dynamic equilibrium for 40 interacting players is impossible because the number of possible industry states explodes ([Pakes–McGuire, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0304.pdf)). Two practical shortcuts exist. One is *oblivious* or *partially oblivious equilibrium*, in which a handful of dominant firms are tracked exactly and the fringe only on average ([Weintraub, Benkard & Van Roy](https://web.stanford.edu/~lanierb/research/Markov_Perfect_Industry_EMA.pdf); [Benkard, Jeziorski & Weintraub](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/3867/markov_perfect.pdf)). It can be solved offline and shipped as lookup tables. The other is *quantal level-k* reasoning, in which players think one or two steps ahead and make small random errors. It predicted initial human play best across 10 datasets ([Wright & Leyton-Brown](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~kevinlb/papers/2017-GEB-PredictingHumanBehavior.pdf)). The notes' own back-of-envelope estimate (not a measurement) puts closed-form triggers, bargaining splits and global-game cutoffs at under a second for thousands of runs. A damped quantal best-response step each quarter takes seconds. Tree search per decision is infeasible except for a single "advise me" player. **Equilibrium selection should be an explicit scenario switch**, for example "arms race" versus "tacit restraint," not something hidden inside the engine.

## Credit and contagion modules decide who is left holding the loss

### Different firm types need different default models

**What they are.** *Structural models* (Merton, Black–Cox, Leland; Moody's KMV) treat equity as an option on the firm's assets. They measure "distance to default," meaning how many standard deviations asset value sits above debt ([Merton 1974](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1974.tb03058.x)). *Reduced-form models* read the market-implied default rate straight from credit default swap (CDS) or bond spreads ([Duffie & Singleton](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/12.4.687)). They match market prices but explain nothing about causes. *Credit scoring* (Altman Z, hazard models) needs audited ratios from mature industries ([Shumway](https://doi.org/10.1086/209665)), so it fits loss-making hyper-growth firms badly. A *liquidity-runway* model asks whether cash plus committed funding covers the contractual payments due this quarter.

**Public data fits only some firms.** For listed investment-grade names, distance-to-default can be computed from equity prices and filings, cross-checked against CDS. Off-balance-sheet leases and guarantees must be added to debt, or risk is understated. For high-yield neoclouds, the CDS market gives the anchor. CoreWeave's 5-year CDS was reported at **about 1,314bp, implying roughly a 68% five-year default probability**, and Oracle's at a record **about 356bp** ([Disruption Banking](https://www.disruptionbanking.com/2026/11/25/why-are-ai-credit-default-swaps-hitting-record-spreads/); [BigGo Finance](https://finance.biggo.com/news/327572f9-1422-43d5-98bb-b93416d5648b)). With 40% recovery, these imply annual default intensities of about 22% and 6%. They are *risk-neutral* probabilities, which normally overstate real-world default rates, so the tool should offer a risk-premium haircut slider rather than present them as forecasts. Market spreads already price contagion fears, so the network layer must not count contagion a second time on top of them. **Private labs need the runway model.** OpenAI's roughly **$1.4T of multi-year compute commitments** (Oracle $300B, Microsoft $250B, Amazon $38B, CoreWeave $22.4B) are reported, not audited ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/openai-is-the-2025-yahoo-finance-company-of-the-year-120054312.html)). Valuations are circular because they are set by the same counterparties, and the cancellation terms of the largest contracts are unknown. So the engine should define default as cash plus committed funding falling below contractual payments, and treat funding-market closure as a regime switch.

### Financing vehicles fail when their tenant fails, just as the collateral is worth least

GPU-backed financing vehicles (SPVs) should be modelled as project-finance waterfalls, not as firms. Lease income comes in, debt service goes out, and a coverage covenant triggers a cash sweep or default. The rating agencies are stretching existing frameworks. KBRA sizes data-centre debt off a sustainable net cash flow with stressed tenant default ([KBRA methodology](https://www.kbra.com/publications/DJRqYcWm/kbra-releases-data-center-abs-global-rating-methodology?format=web)). Its June 2026 note on GPU re-leasing warns that a cluster's economic relevance can decline far faster than its physical life, and that custom TPUs carry more re-leasing risk than Nvidia GPUs ([KBRA](https://www.kbra.com/publications/BhxpJMrf/ai-compute-financings-evaluating-re-leasing-risk-for-gpus-and-tpus?format=web)). The central modelling problem is **"wrong-way" risk**, borrowed from aircraft leasing: the vehicle defaults when its tenant does, which is also when GPU resale values are lowest. Reported calibration anchors are initial loan-to-values of 50–70% and residual values of about 40% for H100-class and 16% for A100-class chips ([Haircut Substack](https://nodiligence.substack.com/p/who-carries-the-gpu-depreciation), secondary). Useful lives differ too: Amazon cut server lives from 6 to 5 years, Meta uses 5.5, and Burry argues the economic life is 2–3 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/14/ai-gpu-depreciation-coreweave-nvidia-michael-burry.html)). The engine should link GPU residual value to its own simulated rental-price index, and should value guarantees, such as Meta's residual value guarantee on the 80/20 Hyperion JV with Blue Owl ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/10/meta-blue-owl-capital-develop-hyperion-data-center/)), only in futures where the guarantor is itself solvent. There is **no loan-level default history** for GPU-backed debt. This is the single largest calibration gap in the credit layer.

### Network clearing shows how losses travel

**What they are.** *Eisenberg–Noe* clearing solves for what every firm can pay when each firm's ability to pay depends on what it receives from the others ([Eisenberg & Noe](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.47.2.236.9835)). *Rogers–Veraart* adds bankruptcy costs, so defaults destroy value ([Rogers & Veraart](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1120.1569)). *DebtRank* spreads distress through equity losses before anyone defaults, such as Nvidia's stakes losing value ([Battiston et al.](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep00541)). *Fire-sale* models capture forced selling that depresses collateral prices for everyone ([Greenwood, Landier & Thesmar](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2014.02.008)).

**Evidence.** Without bankruptcy costs or fire sales, network amplification is often small ([Glasserman & Young](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2014.02.006)). The AI network has the features that create amplification: concentrated exposures, guarantees and funding that depends on sentiment. Van Nieuwerburgh and Su's 2026 NBER paper finds that **removing contractual payment spillovers among key AI firms cuts private losses by about 30%**. They also find that equity cushions are large today and that their calibrated model "supports a large compute expansion" ([NBER w36032](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36032); abstract only, so the method is unconfirmed). The BIS finds spillovers limited so far but rising with issuance ([BIS Bulletin 140](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-140-building-ai-credit-structures-and-spillovers.pdf)). The Fed's November 2026 Financial Stability Report reportedly found that **87% of survey respondents named AI as a top risk**, and that the largest US banks have close to $1T of exposure to large tech, data-centre developers, financing vehicles, private-credit vehicles and utilities ([Fed FSR](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20261113.pdf); [American Banker](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/fed-warns-of-financial-risks-from-ai-driven-market-optimism)). The IMF's April 2026 GFSR sketches $2.9T of data-centre capex through 2028 with about $800B from private bilateral credit, as an illustration rather than a forecast ([IMF GFSR](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).

**Vendor financing makes Nvidia a transmission channel, not just a revenue loser.** Lucent's vendor-financing losses crystallised when customers such as Winstar failed: Winstar missed a $75M payment against a Lucent facility of up to $2B ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1433361/winstar-misses-75m-payment-may-file-chapter-11.html)). Nvidia's exposure is reportedly larger in absolute terms and takes the form of guarantees rather than loans. It includes a conditional obligation to buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity, initially $6.3B through April 2032, and a **reported maximum guarantee exposure of about $118B**, much of it tied to OpenAI's Ohio campus ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/08/17/nvidia-lines-up-105-billion-for-openai-heres-what-it-means-for-investors/); [Cape Fear Advisors](https://capefearadvisors.substack.com/p/nvidia-the-circle-draws-in); both secondary). Lucent's customers were many small carriers, whose defaults were largely independent. Nvidia's exposures are concentrated in a few large counterparties that depend on each other, so **default correlation is the dominant parameter**. A one-factor model tied to the AI demand path, with network clearing added on top, captures it.

**Feasibility.** A 40-node clearing step converges in at most 40 rounds, typically 2–6. With 20–50 financing waterfalls and a DebtRank pass, the notes estimate this layer at roughly 0.3–2 seconds across 2,000 futures with background threads (an engineering estimate, not a measurement). Heavy steps such as maximum-likelihood fitting of structural parameters should run offline, with the resulting parameters shipped to the browser each quarter. With 2,000 futures, a 5% default probability carries a standard error of about 0.5 percentage points. A 1% tail probability is far noisier, so tail outputs need extra sampling of the demand-collapse scenarios.

## Supply, power and China work as ceilings, not forecasts

**Bottleneck models** work out the maximum number of accelerators the supply chain can make. They multiply TSMC CoWoS packaging wafers by packages per wafer, yield and product mix, then cross-check against HBM memory supply, substrates and power, and **take the minimum as the binding constraint**. Sell-side Nvidia models work this way. Morgan Stanley reportedly projects about 910k CoWoS-L units in 2027, implying roughly 52% higher Nvidia revenue ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/amd-epyc-venice-to-outpace-nvidia-vera-cpus-2027/)). The method is trivial to run in a browser, but its inputs are soft. TSMC does not publish CoWoS capacity. Analyst estimates for end-2026 range from 105k wafers per month (JPMorgan, Nov 2025) to 130k and 120–140k, **a spread of up to about 40% for the same date**. Part of the spread is definitional: TSMC-only versus including outsourced packagers, and installed versus target capacity ([JPM via X](https://x.com/jukan05/status/1989479922910597530); [VLSI.kr](https://www.vlsi.kr/en/cowos-price-hike-mb-en/); [EffectStory](https://effectstory.com/en/articles/cowos-capacity-tsmc-roadmap-ase-expansion-2026)). Aggregators expect CoWoS capacity to grow about 70% in 2027, against 45–50% for HBM and 25–30% for substrates, which means **the binding constraint is shifting toward memory** ([EffectStory](https://effectstory.com/en/articles/cowos-capacity-tsmc-roadmap-ase-expansion-2026)). SK hynix says DRAM and HBM are effectively sold out through 2029, with long-term agreements covering more than half of 2027–28 capacity ([Korea JoongAng Daily](https://koreajoongangdaily.com/business/sk-hynix-says-dram-hbm-booked-through-2029-as-profit-soars-to-62b/12942008)). Memory makers' disclosures of these agreements are therefore a good leading indicator. Capacity inputs should be ranges with an explicit source date, not single numbers.

**Power models** come in three types, and the engine should reconcile them by region. The first is bottom-up from chips: units × power per chip × overhead × cooling efficiency. This is LBNL's method, which put US data centres at 176 TWh in 2023 (4.4% of electricity) and 325–580 TWh by 2028, and at 11.8% of US electricity by 2030 in its 2025 update ([LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/us_data_center_energy_usage_report_lbnl-2001637.pdf); [DOE](https://www.energy.gov/documents/united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report-2025-update)). The second sums utility large-load requests and haircuts them for duplicates. Grid Strategies' 166 GW five-year US peak-growth forecast includes about 90 GW of data centres, which it says is likely overstated by about 25 GW because developers apply to several utilities at once ([Grid Strategies](https://gridstrategiesllc.com/wp-content/uploads/Grid-Strategies-National-Load-Growth-Report-2025.pdf?stream=top)). The third is supply-side: interconnection queues with historical completion rates. The global picture is a puzzle the tool should surface rather than resolve. The IEA's 2030 global estimate has barely moved, from about 945 TWh in 2025 to about 1,000 TWh in November 2026 ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/data-center-electricity-demand-iea.html)). Over the same period, US utility forecasts have roughly quadrupled since 2023 ([Latitude Media](https://www.latitudemedia.com/news/one-us-load-growth-estimate-quadrupled-in-just-two-years/)). Consultancy GW-to-dollars models give context but not forecasts. McKinsey projects $6.7T of cumulative data-centre capex to 2030 and about 220 GW of demand ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-cost-of-compute-a-7-trillion-dollar-race-to-scale-data-centers)). Bain says $2T a year of new revenue would be needed to fund about 200 GW ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/about/media-center/press-releases/20252/$2-trillion-in-new-revenue-needed-to-fund-ais-scaling-trend---bain--companys-6th-annual-global-technology-report/)). Epoch's trend-plus-constraints analysis finds 2e29 FLOP training runs feasible by 2030 ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/publications/can-ai-scaling-continue-through-2030)).

**China and sovereign demand** need their own blocks, because the sources conflict sharply. Estimates of Nvidia's China share of AI accelerators range from Jensen Huang's "zero" to Bernstein's ~8% and IDC's 34% ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/huawei-expects-12-billion-in-ai-chip-revenue-this-year-as-nvidias-china-market-share-hits-zero); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/market-nvidia-once-owned-slipping-211700421.html)). Nvidia reportedly shipped $2.4B of Hopper products to China in its October quarter but guides to no China data-centre revenue ([Guru3D](https://www.guru3d.com/story/nvidia-q3-fy2027-usd-1158-billion-revenue-data-center-usd-1076-billion-usd-130-billion-q4/)). The CFR puts Huawei's 2026 AI compute at under 4% of Nvidia's ([CFR](https://cfr.org/articles/chinas-ai-chip-deficit-why-huawei-cant-catch-nvidia-and-us-export-controls-should-remain)). The recommended structure is a supply-constrained China block (SMIC wafers × dies × yield, capped by available HBM) plus an export-policy switch with three settings: none, H20-class or H200-class. Sovereign demand should be a project ledger with a realisation haircut. The gap is large: ABI counts 1.3 GW of dedicated sovereign capacity in 2026, against announced campuses such as Stargate UAE's planned 5 GW, of which 200 MW comes online in 2026 ([ABI Research](https://abiresearch.com/blog/ai-data-center-demand-growth); [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/?p=787966)).

The architecture follows directly from that scorecard. Each quarter of each simulated future passes through the layers in order, and the results feed into the next quarter.

| Layer | What it does | Methods | Outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Inputs and nowcast | Estimates the current quarter and draws external drivers | BSTS/Kalman nowcast; Farmer–Lafond token-price walk; decaying demand growth; regime-switching signal | Starting state; drawn demand, price and rate paths |
| 2. Ceilings | Caps what can be built | CoWoS × yield, HBM bits, GW energised by region; China supply block; sovereign ledger with haircut | Maximum accelerators and GW per quarter |
| 3. Agent decisions | Each of ~40 firms decides | Hyperscaler preemption/attrition with competition neglect and peer term; prospect-theory labs; ambiguity-averse lenders; Nvidia repeated-game guarantees and pricing against in-house chips; damped quantal best response | Orders, capex, prices, funding requests |
| 4. Pipelines | Moves orders into installed capacity | SD delays: ~6–12 months for GPUs, ~24–48 months for shells and power; orderbook state | Deliveries, backlog, utilisation, rental index |
| 5. Settlement and credit | Pays, renegotiates or defaults | SFC ledger; bargaining on contracts; DSCR waterfalls; global-game rollover; Eisenberg–Noe/Rogers–Veraart clearing with guarantees as contingent links; DebtRank; GPU fire sales | Defaults, losses by named holder, spreads |
| 6. Monte Carlo and judgment | Repeats thousands of times and checks plausibility | Seeded runs across background threads; extra tail sampling; reference-class ranges; scenario switches for equilibrium and policy | Fan charts, probabilities, indicator dashboard |

**Calibration** should proceed in four steps. First, set firms' starting balance sheets and contracts directly from filings: 10-Qs, CoreWeave's backlog disclosures ([CoreWeave 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001769628/000176962826000362/coreweave2q26earningspress.htm)), ratings releases and disclosed contract sizes. Firms of the same type share rule templates, which avoids the over-parameterisation trap. Second, run a "history-friendly" test ([Windrum et al.](https://www.lem.sssup.it/WPLem/files/2006-14.pdf)): starting from 2022, the engine should reproduce the observed 2022–26 paths of capex, Nvidia revenue and rental prices. Third, apply pattern-oriented checks ([Grimm et al.](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70161783)), which hold the engine to several stylised facts at different scales rather than one curve. Examples are a chip cycle of +35% then roughly −35%, supplier drawdowns of about −15% for a diversified, low-vendor-finance supplier and about −60–75% for a concentrated vendor-financer, an orderbook peaking near 70% before a crash, and take-or-pay exposure resolved mostly by settlement. Simple likelihood-based Bayesian estimation beat the more popular simulated-moment methods in a head-to-head test ([Platt](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/dyncon/v113y2020ics0165188920300294.html)), but with no future data to fit, scenario ranges plus global sensitivity analysis across the Monte Carlo runs will do most of the work. Fourth, switch contagion off and check that losses fall by an amount in the region of the NBER paper's ~30%. This is a sanity check, not a target.

**Backtesting** applies properly to the statistical layer and loosely to the whole engine. Use rolling-origin tests that refit using only data available at each date ([Hyndman via SAS](https://blogs.sas.com/content/forecasting/2016/06/09/rob-hyndman-on-time-series-cross-validation)), with quarterly origins from about 2015 for chip revenue and hyperscaler capex and horizons of 1, 4 and 8 quarters. Compare against naive and drift benchmarks. Score ranges with CRPS or pinball loss, and binary events such as "capex down year-on-year" with Brier scores. Check that 50% and 90% ranges contain outcomes at their nominal rates, and consider conformal methods for valid intervals ([arXiv 2610.21648](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21648)). Score turning-point episodes separately (2001, 2008–09, 2019, 2022–23), because averages hide exactly the failures that matter ([Hewamalage et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10716)). Then publish timestamped forecasts and score them as quarters arrive. With about a dozen AI-era quarters, every backtest has low statistical power, and the tool should say so.

## Fourteen indicators belong on the tool's front page

Each simulated future should report the indicators below, and the live dashboard should show today's reading next to the simulated distribution. History's most useful *leading* signal was the marginal financier stepping back, so financing indicators come first.

| Indicator | Why it matters | Historical reference | Late-2026 reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyperscaler capex / operating cash flow | Above 100% means outside financing is required | Shale outspent cash flow ~2:1 before its busts | Alphabet ~115%, Amazon ~119% in Q2 2026 ([FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/hyperscalers-tap-external-financing-as-ai-capex-outruns-cash-flow)) — **flashing** |

Public data has holes in four places: measured GPU utilisation, bilateral exposures (the payment matrix has to be rebuilt from press-reported contract sizes and assumed tenors), Epoch's datasets (not reachable from this session), and a compiled base-rate dataset of past tech-capex drawdowns.

## Conclusion

The research changes what the tool should be. It should not be a forecasting machine that tries to beat consensus on Nvidia's 2029 revenue, a contest no method has historically won at turning points. It should be a **stress-testing engine for the capital structure behind the buildout**. Demand curves and technology curves set the ranges. The answers fund managers actually need come from the plumbing: which contracts settle, which lenders roll over, whether Nvidia's guarantees crystallise, and how much stranded capacity exists at the moment financing turns. The analogs suggest an asymmetry worth building in from the start. Because GPUs depreciate in years rather than decades, the "eventually used" consolation of fibre and rail applies mainly to shells, grid connections and power, not to the chips, so losses in an AI bust would fall more heavily and sooner on GPU-collateralised credit than on the property layer.

The method choice also carries a governance lesson. The biggest risk to the tool's credibility is not computing power, since everything recommended here runs in seconds in a browser. It is hidden assumptions: equilibrium choices, contract terms and secondary-source figures presented as facts. Making those visible, as labelled switches, tagged inputs and scored live forecasts, is what separates a scenario tool fund managers can argue with from one that merely restates the bull or bear case of whoever set its defaults.
