## Q1. Analog mechanics: capex scale, financing, utilisation, cost declines, bust triggers, survivors, and supplier revenue

### Takeaway
Across railways, telecom/fibre, shale and shipping, the bust came when **capacity commitments made on extrapolated demand** met **financing that had to be renewed or called**: share calls in 1847, European capital retreating in 1890–93, high-yield and vendor credit in 2000–01, and bank borrowing-base redeterminations in shale in 2015–16. The physical assets mostly survived and were later used heavily. The losers were the leveraged builders, and above all the **equipment suppliers who had financed their own customers**: Lucent and Nortel saw revenue fall by more than 50–75%, while Cisco, which had little vendor financing and a strong enterprise business, saw revenue fall by about 15%. For an Nvidia analog, the deciding variable is **how much demand the supplier is financing or guaranteeing itself**.

### Cited Findings

**British Railway Mania (1830s and 1840s)**
- Odlyzko calls the 1840s mania "by many measures the greatest technology mania in history." He notes that unlike many bubbles it had "many powerful, vocal, and insightful critics", so the mania went ahead despite visible warnings. — [Odlyzko, "Collective hallucinations and inefficient markets: The British Railway Mania of the 1840s"](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/hallucinations.pdf)
- 1830s phase: "at the peak of the financially exuberant early phase, British investors undertook to plow over 8% of their country's GDP into the new railways." Per the search summary, the paper also says that at its height the 1840s mania drew more than twice that. — [Odlyzko, mania01.pdf](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/%7Eodlyzko/doc/mania01.pdf)
- A 2008 Odlyzko talk puts 1840s railway investment at about £200 million, which it equates to about $6 trillion today or "40% of GDP". It estimates that about one-third, roughly $2T of the $6T, was lost to investors. **This is very likely cumulative authorised or committed capital over several years, not annual spending, and so is not comparable to an annual capex/GDP ratio [verify].** — [Odlyzko, GMU 2008 talk](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/talks/gmu2008.pdf)
- Revenue versus expectations: industry revenues rose from £4.54M (year to June 1843) to £6.21M (1845), while investors' implied 1850 expectations were £30–50M. That is a **roughly 5–8x gap between implied and actual revenue**, which is the railway version of the "$600B question". Route mileage was 2,240 in 1844 and 3,945 in 1847. — [Odlyzko papers via search summary](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/%7Eodlyzko/doc/mania08.pdf)
- Odlyzko describes the 1860s British mania as an investment disaster that nonetheless gave the country a nationwide network, with capital investment of 15–20% of GDP (likely cumulative). He calls the 1830s mania "giant, wildly speculative, and successful." — [Odlyzko, "This Time Is Different…" (B.E. J. Econ. Analysis & Policy 2010)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bpj:bejeap:v:10:y:2010:i:1:n:60); [UMN experts page](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/this-time-is-different-an-example-of-a-giant-wildly-speculative-a/)

**US railroads (1865–1897)**
- US rail mileage roughly doubled from about 35,000 miles (1865) to more than 74,000 (1873). Jay Cooke & Co. collapsed in 1873 on its Northern Pacific bet, and 65 railroads defaulted on bonds between 1873 and 1879. **[secondary]** — [Fabricated Knowledge, "The Great Railroad Buildout"](https://www.fabricatedknowledge.com/p/lessons-from-history-the-great-railroad); [Long Depression (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Depression)
- Financing: much of the construction was paid for by British and German investors seeking the 7–8% yields on US railroad bonds. Foreign holdings reached about $3B, around a quarter of US railroad capital. European capital pulled back after the 1890 Baring crisis, *before* the default wave that began with the Philadelphia & Reading in February 1893. **[secondary]** — [MarketHistories, Panic of 1893](https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-panic-of-1893-how-silver-and-railroad-debt-sparked-americas-worst-depression-yet); [Panic of 1893 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1893)
- 1893–94 receiverships: estimates range from more than 15% of operating mileage (132 companies and about 27,570 miles in 1893 alone) to roughly a quarter or a third of mileage by end-1894 (153 railroads, about $2.5B of capital). Union Pacific entered receivership in October 1893 and was reorganised in 1897. The spread in estimates reflects different windows and denominators. — [Panic of 1893 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1893); [MarketHistories](https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-panic-of-1893-how-silver-and-railroad-debt-sparked-americas-worst-depression-yet); [The Rails Were Still There](https://iethereum.substack.com/p/the-rails-were-still-there)
- Capex/GDP: railroads are usually quoted at about 6% of GDP at peak. One analysis says US railroad capex *averaged* 2.4% of GDP in the 1870s–1880s. **[secondary; the peak and average figures should not be mixed]** — [Advisor Perspectives, "AI Spending Boom Is Massive But Not Unprecedented" (Oct 2025)](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2025/10/09/ai-spending-boom-massive-not-unprecedented); [Noah Smith, "AI vs. the railroads"](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/ai-vs-the-railroads); [Investment Research Partners chart](https://www.investmentresearchpartners.com/post/chart-of-the-week-8-3-2025)

**Electrification and utility holding companies (1920s–1935)**
- Insull's utility empire was built on heavy leverage through stacked holding companies, sometimes "ten times removed from the operating company." Holding companies were outside state regulation and could issue stocks and bonds freely. The empire declined from 1929 and collapsed in 1932, triggered by the Chicago Rapid Transit bankruptcy. About 600,000 shareholders took large losses. — [EBSCO Research Starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/insull-utilities-trusts-collapse); [Insull Utilities Investment (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insull_Utilities_Investment_Inc.); [American Heritage](https://americanheritage.com/fall-samuel-insull)
- By 1932, three super-holding companies (United Corp., Insull, Electric Bond & Share) controlled nearly half of US electricity generation. Congress responded with PUHCA in 1935, which required SEC registration and gave the SEC power to break up uneconomic holding companies. — [Electric Bond and Share (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Bond_and_Share_Company); [PBS Frontline timeline](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/blackout/regulation/timeline.html)
- Lesson: the operating assets (power plants and grids) stayed productive. What failed was the **financial superstructure (pyramided leverage)**. That is the closest analog to SPV, JV and neocloud layering on top of hyperscaler demand. — inference supported by the above sources

**Telecom/fibre boom (1996–2002)**
- US telecom capex peaked at about $120B in 2000 (nominal), about $213B in today's dollars. — [Fabricated Knowledge, "Rise and Fall of the Telecom Bubble"](https://www.fabricatedknowledge.com/p/lessons-from-history-the-rise-and); [Quartz, "How AI debt compares to dot-com era telecom borrowing"](https://qz.com/ai-debt-dot-com-telecom-borrowing-comparison-050526)
- Telecom capex is usually quoted at about 1–1.2% of US GDP at peak. **[secondary]** — [Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2025/10/09/ai-spending-boom-massive-not-unprecedented)
- Capex/revenue: the telecom capex-to-revenue ratio in the fibre bubble was about **4x** (Azeem Azhar via Derek Thompson). Compare 2025 AI data-centre spending of about $400B against about $60B of AI revenue, roughly **6–7x**. **[secondary]** — [Derek Thompson, "Why AI Is Not a Bubble*"](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/why-ai-is-not-a-bubble)
- Utilisation: estimates of lit fibre conflict. One source says less than 5% of boom-era fibre was ever lit, another puts unused fibre at 85–95% of about 80M fibre-miles, and a third cites analysts saying about one-tenth was lit. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, not audited data. — [Technostatecraft, "Dark Fiber—an Archaeology of the Dot-Com Bubble"](https://www.technostatecraft.com/p/dark-fiberan-archaeology-of-the-dot); [Princeton/Starr, "The Great Telecom Implosion" (2002)](https://www.princeton.edu/~starr/articles/articles02/Starr-TelecomImplosion-9-02.htm); [IWP Finance case study](https://iwpfinance.com/case-studies/telecom-fiber-bubble-2000)
- Defaults: Global Crossing filed in January 2002 with $22.4B of assets and $12.4B of debt. WorldCom filed in July 2002 with $107B of assets and $41B of debt, by then the 23rd telecom bankruptcy of the wave. FCC Chairman Powell told the Senate in 2002 that much of the sector's debt would never be repaid. About $2T of telecom market cap disappeared, out of a $7T total stock-market decline. — [Quartz](https://qz.com/ai-debt-dot-com-telecom-borrowing-comparison-050526); [Global Crossing (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Crossing); [Princeton/Starr](https://www.princeton.edu/~starr/articles/articles02/Starr-TelecomImplosion-9-02.htm); [Telecoms crash (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecoms_crash)
- Accounting: WorldCom began treating operating expenses (line costs) as capex in late 2000, and almost every major telecom had to restate its 1999–2000 numbers. — [Fabricated Knowledge](https://www.fabricatedknowledge.com/p/lessons-from-history-the-rise-and)
- OECD post-mortem working paper: [OECD Economics Dept WP 361, "After the Telecommunications Bubble" (2003)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2003/06/after-the-telecommunications-bubble_g17a1480/311813664474.pdf) (not read in full)

**Equipment suppliers (the Nvidia analog)**
- Vendor financing commitments were about $8.1B at Lucent (earlier 2001 reports said $7–8B), about $3.1B committed at Nortel with about $1.4B drawn, and about $2.4B of customer loans at Cisco. McKinsey estimated about **$25.6B** of vendor financing across nine suppliers by end-2000. **[secondary; check against filings]** — [Michel Johannsen, "Vendor Financing Loops: What 1999 Telecom Tells Us About 2026 AI"](https://www.micheljohannsen.com/blog/vendor-financing-loops-1999-vs-2026); [Newsweek, "The Stupid Loan Bubble"](https://www.newsweek.com/stupid-loan-bubble-146353)
- Lucent's revenue fell from about $30B to about $12B within two years. After 2003 it stabilised at less than 25% of its 1999 peak. Lucent had a habit of discounting to pull orders into the quarter, which left customers with excess equipment inventory and led to sharp order cuts later. That is a **demand pull-forward mechanism**. — [Lazonick & March, "The Rise and Fall of Lucent Technologies"](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/22012/1/lazonick-march_lucent_final_20100410.pdf); [American Affairs, "Who Lost Lucent?"](https://americanaffairsjournal.org/2020/08/who-lost-lucent-the-decline-of-americas-telecom-equipment-industry/)
- Cisco's revenue went from $22.3B (FY2001) to $18.9B in both FY2002 and FY2003, about −15%. Its fiscal-2001 inventory write-off of roughly $2.2–2.5B (sources differ) is described as the largest in business history, and it laid off 8,500 staff. Cisco held up better because of its enterprise business while optical rivals (Lucent, Nortel, Alcatel) shrank. — [Carpenter & Lazonick, INET WP 202, "Cisco's Transformation"](https://www.ineteconomics.org/uploads/papers/WP_202-Carpenter-and-Lazonick-Cisco-final.pdf); [Globe and Mail (2002)](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/rob-magazine/the-worst-is-not-over-in-telecom-billions-more-in-write-offs-loom-as-backlogs-of-components-pile-up/article4132922/)
- Industry inventory charges were $5.4B in 2001. Cisco disclosed up to $850M of purchase commitments to suppliers under long-term agreements, a maximum exposure rather than a write-off estimate. Nortel wrote down inventory in Q2 and Q3 2001 after inventories grew relative to sales. — [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/rob-magazine/the-worst-is-not-over-in-telecom-billions-more-in-write-offs-loom-as-backlogs-of-components-pile-up/article4132922/)
- Nortel's quarterly revenue fell through 2001–02 to $2.8B in one 2002 quarter. Its market value went from $136B (December 1999) to $14B (April 2002) per CNN, though peak figures vary by source and date. — [Light Reading, "Decline & Fall of Nortel"](https://www.lightreading.com/optical-networking/the-decline-fall-of-nortel-networks); [CNN Money, Feb 2002](https://money.cnn.com/2002/02/12/investing/q_nortel/)
- Industry-wide, communications equipment revenue fell 27% year on year in Q2 2002, from $37.3B a year earlier (Synergy Research). — [Telecompaper](https://www.telecompaper.com/news/cisco-market-leader-total-communicatons-equipment-market--303282)
- One source claims Nortel's bad loans rose from 25.5% to 80% of its vendor loans between end-2000 and end-2001. **[unverified; single secondary source]** — [search summary of commentary](https://brendonbeebe.substack.com/p/everyones-asking-the-wrong-question)

**Shale (2010–2020)**
- Surviving shale gas producers outspent their cash flow about 2:1 in both 2015 and 2016. — [Oilprice.com](https://oilprice.com/Energy/Energy-General/Why-Cheap-Shale-Gas-Will-End-Soon.html)
- More than 100 producer bankruptcies in 2015–16 (Haynes and Boone count), and a later tally of 172 driller bankruptcies with about $98.5B of debt since 2015. Growth was debt-financed and the oil price collapse triggered the bankruptcies. **[secondary; not checked against Haynes Boone]** — [Kirk Coburn, "US Shale Industry: From Boom to Bust" (2020)](https://kirkcoburn.com/2020/08/27/the-us-shale-industry-from-boom-to-bust/); [EUCI (2019)](https://www.euci.com/shale-drillers-continue-to-burn-cash-in-the-q1-of-2019-as-they-face-negative-cash-flow/); [Oil Change International](https://oilchange.org/blogs/us-shale-capital-starting-to-dry-up/)

**Shipping (dry bulk 2003–2016)**
- Drewry data in SEC prospectuses shows the dry bulk orderbook rising from 58.3% of the existing fleet (March 2008, by dwt) to 62.0% (June 2008) and **69.8% (September 2008, 288.1M dwt)**, just before the freight collapse. Martin Stopford (November 2008) put it at 47% versus 15% at the start of the boom, and TradeWinds later cited an 80% peak, so the measures differ. — [Star Bulk Carriers F-3/A (2008), SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001386716/000091957408005968/d904060a_f-3.htm); [Paragon Shipping 424B3 (2008), SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001401112/000091957408003474/d885089_424b-3.htm); [Stopford, Bayes Business School](https://www.bayes.citystgeorges.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0014/34601/Martin-Stopford-5-Biennial.pdf); [Splash247 on orderbook falling below 10%](https://splash247.com/dry-bulk-orderbook-plummets-10-extant-fleet-first-time-since-2002/)

### Inferences
- **The capex-to-current-revenue ratio is the cleanest comparable metric across eras**: railway implied/actual revenue of about 5–8x (1840s), telecom capex/revenue of about 4x, and AI 2025 data-centre capex/AI revenue of about 6–7x. On this measure AI sits at or above the telecom peak, even though capex/GDP is less extreme than the railway peaks.
- Suppliers' revenue drawdowns depended on (a) how much revenue came from customers they had financed, (b) inventory and purchase commitments, and (c) whether they had a diversified non-boom business. Roughly: Cisco −15%, Lucent −60% to −75%, Nortel similar or worse. For Nvidia the model should parameterise all three: the share of revenue from neoclouds and AI labs it has financed or guaranteed, its purchase obligations to TSMC and HBM suppliers, and its non-hyperscaler revenue.
- In every case the **physical capacity was eventually used**. Fibre was lit over the following decade, rail networks became national assets, and power grids stayed in use. The losses fell on the capital structure and equity, not on long-run demand. A simulation should separate "is demand eventually there?" from "does the timing match the financing?".
- GPUs differ from fibre and rails in one key way: they **depreciate or become obsolete in about 3–6 years**, compared with decades for fibre and rails. So "build now, demand arrives later" works much worse for AI accelerators than for fibre. A GPU glut turns into write-offs rather than dormant assets that can be relit cheaply. Powered shells, grid connections and power contracts look more like the fibre conduit: long-lived and eventually used.

- No primary FCC figures for lit-fibre share. The widely repeated "about 2.7% lit" figure (2001–02) could not be sourced in this session.
- Exact Lucent and Nortel annual revenue series were not confirmed from 10-Ks. Background-knowledge figures, unverified: Lucent about $33.8B in FY2000, $21.3B in FY2001 and $12.3B in FY2002; Nortel about $30B in 2000, $17.5B in 2001 and $10.6B in 2002.

- Electrification capex/GDP for the 1920s and utility capacity utilisation were not found.
- Freight-rate collapse figures for 2008 (Baltic Dry Index peak to trough) were not retrieved.

## Q2. Forecasting methods used at the time and how they failed

### Takeaway
The forecasts that drove the overbuilds were **naïve exponential extrapolations** from a short, early, small-base window. Examples are "internet traffic doubles every 100 days" and railway revenue projections of 5–8x actual revenue. **Supply-side plans were added up as though each builder would win its share**, which hid the collective overcapacity. Critics were present and correct, as with Odlyzko in 1998–2000 and critics of the 1840s railways, but were ignored.

### Cited Findings
- The "traffic doubles every 100 days" claim traces to WorldCom/UUNET, including a November 1997 WorldCom press release. It had a factual basis only in a short 1995–96 window when the network was tiny. Odlyzko and Coffman measured actual growth of about 100% per year, a doubling roughly once a year. — [Odlyzko, "Internet traffic growth: Sources and implications"](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/itcom.internet.growth.pdf); [Odlyzko & Coffman, "Internet growth: Is there a 'Moore's Law' for data traffic?"](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/internet.moore.pdf)
- Odlyzko wrote that the myth "was often a clincher for new venture business plans." WorldCom officials kept repeating it as late as 2002, and later work found growth had slowed to 50–60% per year. — [Odlyzko, "Bubbles, gullibility, and other challenges…"](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/mania03.pdf); [Odlyzko CRTC 2009 report](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/misc/crtc-2009-report.pdf)
- The telecom bubble was fed by deregulation (the Telecommunications Act of 1996) together with the 100-day doubling forecast, which led to overbuilding of long-haul fibre. — [IWP Finance](https://iwpfinance.com/case-studies/telecom-fiber-bubble-2000); [Forbes, "All Aboard the Bubble Express" (2010)](https://www.forbes.com/forbes/2010/0315/technology-bubbles-fiber-optics-social-networking/); [Techdirt, "What Bandwidth Crunch?" (2007)](https://www.techdirt.com/2007/09/18/what-bandwidth-crunch/)
- In the 1840s railway mania, implied revenue expectations for 1850 (£30–50M) were set against actual revenues of £4.5–6.2M. Odlyzko frames this as a "collective hallucination" that persisted despite insightful critics. — [Odlyzko, hallucinations paper](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/hallucinations.pdf); [GMU 2008 talk](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/talks/gmu2008.pdf)
- A modern analog argument: projections of exploding AI inference demand are being repeated in investor decks the way "100 days" was, and the "need for speed" is presented as inevitable. **[opinion]** — [Future Forwarded, "From Fiber to Gigawatts"](https://futureforwarded.substack.com/p/from-fiber-to-gigawatts-the-myth); [SemiWiki, "2000 Zero Zero, Party Over"](https://semiwiki.com/artificial-intelligence/376608-2000-zero-zero-party-over-oops-out-of-time)
- Lucent's practice of discounting to pull future orders into the current quarter inflated measured demand, so suppliers' own order books overstated end demand. — [Lazonick & March](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/22012/1/lazonick-march_lucent_final_20100410.pdf)

### Inferences
- Failure mode 1 is **extrapolating early-phase growth rates**. Traffic did grow about 100% per year, which is very fast, but plans assumed roughly 1,000% per year (doubling every 100 days is about 12x a year). An AI model should not take 2024–26 token-growth rates (often quoted at 10x or more per year) as the steady state. It should decay them and test sensitivity.
- Failure mode 2 is **confusing volume growth with revenue growth**. Telecom traffic did grow, but bandwidth prices fell faster than volume rose, so revenue lagged. Falling AI token prices (falling cost per token, cheaper open-weight models, as Slok notes in Q4) are the direct analog.
- Failure mode 3 is **fallacy of composition in supply plans**: each carrier's plan assumed it would win share. AI capacity plans by hyperscalers, neoclouds, sovereigns and labs should be summed and compared against one consistent demand projection.
- Failure mode 4 is **supplier-reported demand as a signal**. Book-to-bill and backlog figures were inflated by vendor financing and quarter-end pull-forward.

### Gaps
- Contemporaneous sell-side or consultancy demand models from 1999–2000 (for example, RHK and KMI fibre-demand forecasts) were not retrieved.
- Specific 1840s demand models, such as railway prospectus traffic projections or Hudson's projections, were not retrieved beyond Odlyzko's aggregate revenue comparison.

## Q3. Quantitative early-warning indicators, with evidence

### Takeaway
The indicators that turned before or at historical peaks were: (1) **capex/revenue and capex/operating cash flow above 100%**, which means external financing is needed; (2) **supplier-provided financing and extended payment terms** (vendor loans, DSO, guarantees); (3) **supplier inventory and purchase commitments rising faster than sales**; (4) **orderbook/fleet or committed capacity versus installed base** (shipping peaked at about 70% just before the 2008 crash); (5) **foreign or marginal capital retreating and credit spreads widening** (Baring 1890 before the 1893 defaults, high-yield telecom in 2000–01, Oracle CDS in 2026); (6) **accounting changes that flatter earnings** (WorldCom's opex-to-capex reclassification; 2020s depreciation-life extensions). By late 2026, several of these are flashing for AI.

### Cited Findings — historical indicator evidence
- **Capex/revenue:** telecom was about 4x at the bubble. Oracle's capex reached 46.5% of revenue over four quarters, higher than WorldCom's ratio. **[secondary]** — [Derek Thompson](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/why-ai-is-not-a-bubble); [Behind the Balance Sheet, "Investment Excitement Ratio"](https://behindthebalancesheet.substack.com/p/the-investment-excitement-ratio-ee5)
- **Vendor financing share:** about $25.6B across nine suppliers by end-2000 (McKinsey), with Lucent at $7–8B against about $30B of revenue, roughly 25% of a year's revenue. **[secondary]** — [Johannsen](https://www.micheljohannsen.com/blog/vendor-financing-loops-1999-vs-2026); [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/stupid-loan-bubble-146353)
- **Inventory/sales:** Nortel's inventories grew relative to sales before its 2001 write-downs, Cisco took a roughly $2.2–2.5B write-off, and industry inventory charges were $5.4B in 2001. — [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/rob-magazine/the-worst-is-not-over-in-telecom-billions-more-in-write-offs-loom-as-backlogs-of-components-pile-up/article4132922/); [INET WP 202](https://www.ineteconomics.org/uploads/papers/WP_202-Carpenter-and-Lazonick-Cisco-final.pdf)
- **Orderbook/fleet:** dry bulk was 58% → 62% → 70% (March to September 2008), peaking right before the freight-rate collapse. — [Star Bulk F-3/A (SEC)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001386716/000091957408005968/d904060a_f-3.htm)
- **Marginal-financier retreat:** European capital pulled out of US railroads after the 1890 Baring crisis, ahead of the 1893 default wave. — [MarketHistories](https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-panic-of-1893-how-silver-and-railroad-debt-sparked-americas-worst-depression-yet)
- **Cash burn versus cash flow:** shale producers outspent cash flow about 2:1 through 2015–16, and more than 100 bankruptcies followed. — [Oilprice.com](https://oilprice.com/Energy/Energy-General/Why-Cheap-Shale-Gas-Will-End-Soon.html); [Kirk Coburn](https://kirkcoburn.com/2020/08/27/the-us-shale-industry-from-boom-to-bust/)
- **Accounting changes:** WorldCom capitalised operating costs from late 2000 onward, and almost all major telecoms restated their 1999–2000 accounts. — [Fabricated Knowledge](https://www.fabricatedknowledge.com/p/lessons-from-history-the-rise-and)

### Cited Findings — the same indicators for AI, 2025–2026
- **Capex/OCF above 100%:** in Q2 2026, capex/operating cash flow was about 115% at Alphabet, 119% at Amazon, 95% at Meta and 65% at Microsoft. ## Q4. Published comparisons of the AI boom to past analogs (2024–2026), and key differences

### Takeaway
By late 2026 the debate has moved from "the revenue gap" (Sequoia's $200B, then $600B, then a $3T floor) to **"the financing structure"**: debt issuance, SPVs, vendor guarantees and negative FCF. Central banks (BoE FPC, BIS, IMF) now flag AI valuations and opaque or circular financing as financial-stability risks. Bulls (Goldman's "AI: In a Bubble" report, and others) point to cash-flow-funded spending by highly profitable firms and lower debt/profits than in 2000. Those differences were **true in 2024–25 but have been eroding in 2026** as capex/OCF passed 100%.

### Inferences
- The Perez framework, which holds that the installation period ends in a financial crash followed by a deployment "golden age", fits the evidence. Railways, fibre and electrification all ended with the infrastructure in use but the financiers wiped out. The modelling question is less "bubble or not" than **when the financing turns and how big the capacity overshoot is at that moment**.
- The required-revenue estimates (JPMorgan $650B/yr, Sequoia $3T cumulative-style floor, Goldman PV-positive) differ mostly because of hurdle rates, asset life and terminal-value assumptions. A simulation should expose these as parameters rather than adopt one figure.

## Q5. How a simulation should represent these mechanics

### Takeaway
Model AI infrastructure as a **multi-agent capacity-commitment system with financing constraints and lags**, not a demand-extrapolation curve. Demand is tied to price-elastic usage. Several builders commit capacity independently, with 18–36 month lags. Each builder's financing regime switches (cash flow → bonds → SPVs → supplier support) as capex/OCF rises. Busts are triggered when financing fails or when price falls below cash cost, and supplier revenue is the *derivative* of the capex path, which produces a cliff.

### Cited Findings (mechanics grounded in the analogs)
- **Capacity overshoot from lagged, uncoordinated commitments:** the dry bulk orderbook reached 70% of the fleet before freight rates collapsed ([SEC/Drewry](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001386716/000091957408005968/d904060a_f-3.htm)), and the telecom fibre overbuild left 85–95% unlit ([Technostatecraft](https://www.technostatecraft.com/p/dark-fiberan-archaeology-of-the-dot)).
- **Expectation formation by extrapolation:** the 100-day doubling claim against actual 100%/yr growth ([Odlyzko](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/itcom.internet.growth.pdf)), and railway revenue expectations of about 5–8x actual ([Odlyzko GMU talk](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/talks/gmu2008.pdf)).
- **Financing-regime switch and refinancing walls:** European capital retreated after 1890 and defaults followed in 1893 ([MarketHistories](https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-panic-of-1893-how-silver-and-railroad-debt-sparked-americas-worst-depression-yet)); shale outspent cash flow 2:1 before the bankruptcies ([Oilprice](https://oilprice.com/Energy/Energy-General/Why-Cheap-Shale-Gas-Will-End-Soon.html)); and JPMorgan sees about $4.1T of debt-financed AI capex in 2026–30 ([BoE FPC](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/financial-policy-committee-record/2026/september-2026)).
- **Supplier revenue cliff and pull-forward:** Lucent's revenue went from about $30B to $12B in two years, with discounting pulling orders forward ([Lazonick & March](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/22012/1/lazonick-march_lucent_final_20100410.pdf)). Cisco's revenue fell 15% and its inventory write-off was about $2.2–2.5B ([INET](https://www.ineteconomics.org/uploads/papers/WP_202-Carpenter-and-Lazonick-Cisco-final.pdf)).
- **Leverage layering:** the Insull pyramids were up to 10 levels deep ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/insull-utilities-trusts-collapse)), and the modern equivalent is Meta Hyperion's 80/20 JV with residual value guarantees ([Global Data Center Hub](https://www.globaldatacenterhub.com/p/the-hyperion-financing-is-the-signal)).
- **Price-collapse transmission:** falling token prices and open-weight models weaken the revenue case ([TechCrunch/Slok](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/09/can-ai-answer-the-3-trillion-question/)), and H100 on-demand prices fell 58–75% between 2024 and 2026 ([Shattered.io](https://shattered.io/h100-h200-b200-cloud-gpu-pricing-2026/)).

### Inferences — recommended model components
1. **Demand block:**
   - Generate token or compute demand with a decaying growth rate, for example g(t) = g∞ + (g0 − g∞)·e^(−λt). Do not use a constant exponential: Odlyzko showed real traffic growth was far below the claimed rate and slowed over time, from about 100% to 50–60% per year.
   - Make demand price-elastic. Revenue = volume × price, with price falling with unit cost (Wright's law or a learning rate on cost/token).
   - Track AI *revenue* separately from *compute volume*, because telecom traffic grew while revenue did not keep pace.
2. **Builder agents** (hyperscalers, neoclouds, sovereigns, AI labs):
   - Each agent commits capacity from its own adaptive demand forecast plus a market-share ambition. Summed commitments can exceed demand, which is the fallacy of composition.
   - Use a commitment-to-delivery lag of about 6–12 months for GPUs and 24–48 months for powered shells and grid connections. An "orderbook/installed base" state variable is the shipping-style warning signal.
3. **Financing block:**
   - Each agent has a funding stack: OCF → IG bonds → private credit or SPV → supplier support.
   - The cost and availability of each tier depend on spread variables linked to sector sentiment, utilisation and FCF.
   - Include a **refinancing wall**: SPV and neocloud debt maturities and GPU-collateral loan-to-values set against GPU resale prices that fall with each new generation.
   - Switching regimes on capex/OCF above 100% should raise sensitivity to credit conditions.
4. **Asset-life and obsolescence:**
   - GPUs depreciate over 3–6 years, with an economic-life parameter separate from the accounting life (the Burry gap).
   - Shells and power are long-lived, about 20+ years.
   - In a downturn, GPUs are written off while shells are mothballed and later reused, so stranded-asset losses are highly asymmetric.
5. **Price formation:** spot compute price set by the utilisation-to-price curve (convex near 100% utilisation). Include reserved versus on-demand segments, since the 2025–26 split showed on-demand falling while reserved prices rose.
6. **Supplier block (Nvidia and others):**
   - Supplier revenue is roughly the *change* in installed capacity plus replacement (accelerator dynamics). A capex plateau, not only a fall, causes supplier revenue to stall, and a capex decline causes a cliff.
   - Model vendor financing as a feedback loop: guarantees and receivables raise current revenue but add contingent liabilities that crystallise when customers fail.
   - Add inventory and purchase-commitment state variables (Cisco's write-off).
   - Calibrate drawdowns to history: about −15% for a diversified, low-vendor-finance supplier (Cisco 2001–02), and −60% to −75% for a concentrated, vendor-financing supplier (Lucent and Nortel).
7. **Triggers:**
   - Sample from several triggers: (a) a financing shock, meaning the marginal financier retreats (Baring 1890 / high-yield 2000 / private credit 202x); (b) price falling below cash cost per unit; (c) a revenue-gap recognition event; (d) an accounting or fraud event (WorldCom).
   - The model should let any one trigger start a cascade through the financing and supplier blocks.
8. **Indicator outputs to track in each simulated path:**
   - capex/OCF;
   - capex/AI revenue (historical danger zone ≥4x);
   - committed/installed capacity;
   - supplier DSO, receivables and guarantees as a share of revenue;
   - SPV/off-balance-sheet share of financing;
   - GPU rental price index;
   - utilisation;
   - spreads of the weakest large borrower;
   - gap between depreciation life and economic life.
9. **Calibration anchors:**
   - capex/GDP: about 1% for telecom, 6% or more for railways, 1.2–1.6% for AI in 2024–25, and Brookings' 3.6%/yr forecast for 2025–32;
   - capex/revenue: telecom about 4x, AI about 6–7x in 2025;
   - required revenue: $650B/yr (JPMorgan, 10% IRR);
   - 2026 hyperscaler capex: about $720–745B.

### Gaps
- No published agent-based or system-dynamics model calibrated to the telecom bubble was found in this session. One may exist in the academic literature, for example in system-dynamics treatments of capacity cycles; Sterman's work on boom-and-bust is a likely starting point [not retrieved].
- No quantitative estimate was found of the elasticity of AI usage to token price. It is needed for the demand block.
- Base rates for the lag between the peak of supplier financing and the supplier revenue peak (for example, Lucent's vendor-finance build in 1999–2000 against its revenue peak in FY2000) were not retrieved from filings.
